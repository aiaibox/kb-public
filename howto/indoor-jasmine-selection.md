# Indoor jasmine selection

**Scope:** Which jasmine to buy for indoor fragrance with easy care, how to
contain its size, and how to inspect a plant before buying. Excludes outdoor
landscape use, propagation and soil-mix recipes.

## Conclusion

**Buy *Jasminum sambac* 'Maid of Orleans'.** Ever-blooming in warm conditions,
naturally bushy rather than viny, and holdable at **1–2 ft** with regular pruning
where the species otherwise sits at 3–5 ft.

Care, in full:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Brightest available south- or west-facing window |
| **Water** | Consistently moist spring–autumn; **cut back significantly during winter rest** or roots will rot |
| **Prune** | Head back / tip-pinch immediately after each bloom flush; hard-prune up to one-third or more in spring if leggy |
| **Support** | A **thin** bamboo stake or wire hoop — all jasmines are twiners and cannot grip a smooth thick pole |
| **Pot** | Pot size is itself a growth limiter; don't over-pot if you want it small |

Indoor size-containment ranking, easiest first:

1. **Arabian Jasmine** (*J. sambac*) — shrub-like, easiest
2. **Star Jasmine** (*Trachelospermum jasminoides*) — moderate, responds well to heading back
3. **Pink Jasmine** (*J. polyanthum*) — aggressive vine, needs the most frequent pruning

Runner-up for maximum scent: **'Grand Duke of Tuscany'** — double
carnation-like flowers, most intense scent, but needs more humidity attention.

## Verify

```
On delivery, confirm the pot label reads Jasminum sambac 'Maid of Orleans'
  — single white flowers, bushy habit — and NOT Trachelospermum jasminoides.
Health checklist:
  - Deep, uniform green leaves; no yellowing or chlorosis
  - Leaf undersides free of fine webbing (spider mites) and white cottony
    spots (mealybugs)
  - Stems firm and supple with SHORT internodes; long gaps mean it was grown
    in poor light
  - Roots white to light tan and firm, no sour or swampy odour. Slightly
    root-bound is fine; a hard circling mass is not
Quarantine two weeks away from existing plants; confirm no pests appear.
After the first bloom flush, prune the tips and confirm bushier regrowth
  rather than single long vines.
```

## Facts

- The genus *Jasminum* has **over 200 species, but only ~7–10 thrive** in a
  typical low-humidity, light-limited home. Most jasmine sold as a houseplant is
  one of a handful of viable cultivars.
- Jasmine **tolerates hard pruning**: up to one-third or more in spring.
- **Star Jasmine is not a jasmine.** *Trachelospermum jasminoides* is family
  Apocynaceae. It contains milky latex sap that can irritate skin when pruning.
- **Twining** means the whole stem coils around a support by circular tip
  movement (nutation); climb direction is genetically fixed per species. Jasmines
  have neither tendrils nor adhesive pads, so they need a physical support thin
  enough to encircle.
- Other climbing strategies, for comparison: tendrils (peas, grapes,
  passionflower), adhesive pads or rootlets (ivy, creeping fig), scramblers with
  thorns or hooks (climbing roses, bougainvillea).
- 'Maid of Orleans' has single white flowers and is the cultivar famously used
  for jasmine tea.

## Rejected

| Option | Reason |
|---|---|
| Pink Jasmine (*J. polyanthum*) | Most aggressive vine of the three; takes over indoors without frequent pruning; blooms only late winter–spring |
| Star Jasmine (*T. jasminoides*) | Not a true jasmine; light hog that goes leggy and refuses to bloom without several hours of very bright light; spider-mite magnet in dry indoor air; irritant sap. Only justified with a sunroom-level spot |
| 'Grand Duke of Tuscany' | Best-in-class scent and exotic double flowers, but needs more humidity attention — loses on easy care |
| 'Belle of India' | Exceptionally fragrant but a more vigorous viner, so more structural pruning |
| *J. officinale*, *J. nudiflorum*, Primrose, Downy, Spanish, Italian, Azores, Angel Wing | Large vines or temperate species needing cold winter dormancy; weaken or attract pests kept indoors year-round |
